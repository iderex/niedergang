# Reentries that left evidence on the ground

Validation needs cases where something reached the ground and somebody wrote
down what it was and where it landed. This document is the list of those cases
that could be found from public sources, and its shortness is the finding.

The best available estimate of how much material exists to check a survivability
model against was written by the group that did the laboratory work on several of
the cases below. Excluding Columbia, they estimated that fewer than 250 items had
been recovered in more than forty years of spaceflight, and that fragments from
fewer than a dozen reentries had been analysed in a laboratory [1]. That
sentence, from 2005, bounds what any reentry tool can be validated against, and
nothing found since changes its order of magnitude.

The two lists below are kept apart on purpose. A recovered fragment and a
reentry that produced nothing anybody found are both evidence, and they are not
the same kind of evidence. The second list is the weaker kind and is worth as
much attention, because a footprint that predicts debris where none was found is
wrong in a direction that matters.

Four notes beyond the table.

The two sources for the 1997 Delta II case do not agree on how much was
recovered. The contemporaneous newsletter reports two large fragments, the tank
and the pressurant sphere, and says a very small fragment may have landed near
Tulsa [6]. The 2005 analysis reports four items including the thrust chamber
recovered near Seguin, and gives impact coordinates for all four [1]. The later
and fuller account is used in the table. The disagreement is recorded rather
than smoothed over, because a survey that quietly picks the larger number is a
survey a reader cannot audit.

The Dragon trunk rows are one repeating case rather than three coincidences, and
the operator and the agency have said why. The trunk is composite, which behaves
in reentry heating almost like a thermal protection system, and it is released
before the capsule's deorbit burn, so it stays in orbit and comes down
uncontrolled later. Both parties have said the models that predicted full
burn-up were inadequate [13]. For a survivability tool this is the clearest
public statement that composite structures are where the existing models are
weakest.

Columbia is not an uncontrolled decay from orbit. It was an off nominal entry of
a vehicle that was under control until it broke up, so it does not test the part
of a tool that predicts when and where an abandoned object comes down. What it
tests is everything after breakup: how many fragments, how much mass survives,
and how long and wide the footprint gets. On that it is the only case with
enough recovered material to be statistically useful at all.

The 2024 pallet stanchion is the most useful single row in this table for a
demise model, and it is useful because a prediction was wrong. The pallet was
expected to burn up completely. It did not, the surviving part was identified,
and the agency said it would investigate why it survived and update its
modelling [14]. A case where a model said demise and the ground says otherwise,
with the surviving component named, is worth more than several cases that agreed.

## What was measured about the breakup itself

There is very little, and this section exists because a breakup model can be
checked against nothing else.

The ATV-1 campaign above is the richest optical data set: separation and breakup
altitudes for a large vehicle, observed rather than inferred.

Instrumented reentry is the only source of data from inside a breakup. A
recorder released during the breakup of a cargo vehicle survived to the ocean
and transmitted its gyro, accelerometer, temperature and pressure record by
satellite phone after the reentry of HTV-2 on 2011-03-30 [22], [23]. The same
device flew on ATV-2 on 2011-06-21 and never called home, so that reentry
returned nothing [24]. Later units flew and their data was published together
[25].

The two Delta II reconstructions give breakup altitudes derived from impact
points and tracking rather than assumed: 77.8 km for the second stage in 1997
and 71.8 km for the third stage in 2001 [1]. This matters more than it looks,
because the 78 km breakup altitude that several widely used tools assume rests
on analyses of this kind [1]. A model that assumes 78 km and is validated
against a case whose breakup altitude was reconstructed at 77.8 km has not
tested its assumption; it has met it.

The limiting statement is the one the same authors made: no data had been
collected during the breakup of an unprotected spacecraft, which is why the
recorder was built [1]. Nothing found in this survey shows that gap closed for a
spacecraft as opposed to a cargo vehicle.

## How this list was put together, and what is missing

Every row cites where it came from. Where a source is press reporting rather
than a technical document, the row says so by citing it, and those rows carry
village level positions and no material data. They are in the table because a
recovered piece of a known parent is evidence even when the write up is thin,
and they are not in the table as measurements.

What was read in full: the conference paper [1], which supplied the Delta II
rows, the Columbia figures and the estimate of how little recovered material
exists. Everything else was read as an agency page, a blog post from the agency
running the campaign, a press report, or a paper abstract. The rows sourced from
abstracts and press are marked by their citations rather than by a separate
column, and no material property, temperature or mass in this document comes
from anything other than [1] and [14].

What is missing, and would change this document:

Recoveries documented in languages and archives not searched here. Soviet, Russian
and Chinese recoveries are almost certainly under-represented, and their absence
is a property of this search and not of the world.

Recoveries held privately. An operator who recovers a piece of its own hardware
has no obligation to publish, and at least one of the rows above exists only
because the operator chose to say so.

Anything after the date this file was written. Reentries producing recovered
debris are now frequent enough that a survey goes stale, and the rows from 2020
onward are the ones that will grow.
